# Follow-Up Research Lens: Accessibility & Neurodivergence

**Purpose of this document:** This is a follow-up research pass to `literature-review.md`, which explicitly flagged as a blind spot that none of its seven original lenses considered how the app would need to adapt for users with stuttering, hearing differences, ADHD, autism, dyslexia, or other conditions affecting speech production or auditory/language processing — on top of being a second-language learner. This document is written from a dedicated **accessibility & neurodivergence (speech-language pathology + Universal Design for Learning)** lens to close that gap.

**Method and grounding rule:** Every non-trivial claim below carries a real citation (DOI, URL, or full reference). Where the literature genuinely has little or nothing to say about a specific intersection (e.g., "autism and L2 English intonation training"), this is stated explicitly rather than filled with speculation. Any claim that could not be traced to a citable source is labeled **"assumption — unverified."** As with the base review, this is pure research — **no feature or product decisions are made here.**

---

## 1. Key Findings by Condition

### 1.1 Stuttering (developmental/persistent)

**Confidence: MODERATE-TO-STRONG for prevalence and L1-vs-L2 patterns; WEAK for mechanism.**

- **Adult prevalence:** A 2024 recalculation puts adult stuttering prevalence at **0.96%** (95% CI 0.65–1.44), split into 0.63% overt and 0.33% covert stuttering (Gattie, Lieven & Kluk, 2024, *Journal of Fluency Disorders*, [doi:10.1016/j.jfludis.2024.106086](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfludis.2024.106086)), consistent with the companion systematic review's 0.6–0.7% estimate ([doi:10.1016/j.jfludis.2024.106085](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfludis.2024.106085)) and with ASHA's widely-cited ~1% lifetime figure ([ASHA Fluency Disorders Practice Portal](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/fluency-disorders/)).
- **L2 speaking measurably increases stuttering-like disfluency relative to L1 in most (not all) studies.** A direct experimental study of 28 bilingual persons who stutter found *more* typical and stuttering-like disfluencies in L2 than L1 in both spontaneous and controlled speech tasks, with higher L2 proficiency predicting fewer disfluencies — an attentional-resource account ([PubMed 33965883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33965883/)). A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of pediatric bilingual stuttering similarly found severity was higher in L2 than L1, "reflecting proficiency and dominance effects" ([PMID 40158476](https://www.unboundmedicine.com/medline/citation/40158476/)). A Kurdish-Persian comparative study found overall stuttering-symptom frequency was significantly higher in L2 (Persian) than L1 (Kurdish), though the specific *types* of symptoms differed by language ([Brieflands, *Journal of Health Reports and Technology*](https://brieflands.com/journals/jhrt/articles/166361)).
- **This is not unanimous.** A systematic review of language-related factors in bilingual stuttering found the "most common pattern" is more stuttering in the non-dominant/L2 language, but explicitly notes conflicting studies (Howell et al.; Jayaram 1983) reporting the opposite, with "no scientifically tenable explanation" offered for the reverse pattern in any study ([ScienceDirect S0094730X21000024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094730X21000024)). Treat "L2 increases stuttering" as the evidence-weighted default, not a settled fact.
- **Diagnostic risk specific to bilinguals (not just those who stutter):** Multiple systematic reviews found that applying monolingual-English disfluency norms to bilingual speakers causes systematic misdiagnosis, because bilingual speakers of all kinds show elevated *typical* (non-stuttering) disfluency rates that can exceed monolingual clinical thresholds ([systematic review, PMID 40924645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40924645/); [Eggers, 2025 conference proceedings](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JZQQDVXWYRPJEFYZ73EGNJRH); [Use of Monolingual English Guidelines, doi:10.1558/jmbs.12733](https://doi.org/10.1558/jmbs.12733)). Recommended practice: evaluate disfluency in *all* languages spoken and weight dysrhythmic phonations (blocks/prolongations) and physical concomitants over raw frequency counts, since frequency-based counts are least reliable across bilinguals.
- **SLP recommendations for L2 speakers who stutter:** Fluency gains from treatment delivered in only *one* language reliably generalize to the untreated language in adults — shown in English-Mandarin bilinguals after English-only speech-restructuring treatment ([doi:10.3109/17549507.2014.979874](https://doi.org/10.3109/17549507.2014.979874)), in a 5-participant prolonged-speech study ([doi:10.4103/jisha.jisha_29_18](https://doi.org/10.4103/jisha.jisha_29_18)), and even when controlling for which language (dominant/non-dominant) was treated using combined tDCS+behavioral intervention ([PMC11781365](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11781365/)) — suggesting a general/motor fluency mechanism rather than purely language-specific transfer. ASHA-affiliated guidance explicitly recommends continuing to use/encourage the home language rather than restricting it during treatment, since simultaneous or sequential treatment of both languages produces comparable outcomes ([ASHA Perspectives, doi:10.1044/2022_PERSP-21-00235](https://doi.org/10.1044/2022_PERSP-21-00235)).
- **Digital stuttering intervention apps:** A 2025 pilot validation study of "Eloquent," a guided digital fluency-shaping app, found a 52.7% reduction in Stuttering Severity Index-4 scores after a 15-session intervention with blinded SLP scoring ([arXiv:2503.02743](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02743)) — encouraging, but a small-N pilot (not yet an RCT), and the app teaches specific clinical techniques (light contacts, gentle onsets, pull-outs), not general pronunciation coaching.

### 1.2 Dyslexia

**Confidence: MODERATE.** The phonological mechanism is well-established; direct dyslexic-L2-pronunciation-outcome studies are few but real and specific.

- **Mechanistic link:** L2 learning requires perceiving, attending to, memorizing, and producing new speech sounds — exactly the phonological-awareness and verbal-short-term-memory functions impaired in dyslexia ([Soroli, Szenkovits & Ramus, "Exploring dyslexics' phonological deficit III"](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/soroli10.pdf)). This is not incidental overlap; it is the same cognitive resource on both sides.
- **Oral production is disproportionately harder than written production for dyslexic adults.** A study of 18 native-Spanish-speaking adults with dyslexia learning English found group differences vs. controls in written production (spelling errors), but *significantly larger* differences in oral production (semantic errors, lexical diversity, sentence complexity) — leading the authors to propose a "language spontaneity deficit hypothesis": dyslexic adults are disproportionately affected by the unplanned, real-time nature of speaking specifically, as distinct from writing ([Springer Nature, summarized via springermedicine.com](https://www.springermedicine.com/dyslexia/language-skills-of-adults-with-dyslexia-in-english-as-a-foreign-/50793780)). This is directly relevant to an app whose stated goal is real-time spontaneous speaking rather than scripted read-aloud practice.
- **Heterogeneity, not a single profile:** A study of Italian EFL learners with Specific Learning Difficulties found dyslexic learners produced faster, more consistent speech, while learners with *multiple* co-occurring SpLDs showed increased pausing/hesitation from greater working-memory demand ([doi:10.30687/elle/2280-6792/2026/02/001](https://doi.org/10.30687/elle/2280-6792/2026/02/001)).
- **A specific, actionable, non-obvious finding on training method:** A Finnish ERP (mismatch-negativity) study of 24 dyslexic children found that simple, non-gamified, explicit phonological training increased neural discrimination of English speech sounds *more* than gamified training in the dyslexia group — the opposite pattern held for typically-developing controls — and the benefit was largest for children with the weakest baseline phonological awareness ([Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, doi:10.3389/fnhum.2023.1122886](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2023.1122886)). This directly complicates any assumption that "gamify it" is a universally correct lever for phonological/sound training.
- **Some evidence of a partial compensatory profile:** children with dyslexia may retain heightened sensitivity to some universal phonetic contrasts that are normally lost during typical L1 phonological development (Serniclaes et al., cited in [PMC7033610](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7033610/)) — a nuanced, contested finding, not a blanket "dyslexics are better at accents" claim.
- **Instructional consensus:** explicit, systematic, multisensory instruction (one sound at a time, explicit L1-L2 contrastive comparison, trace/say/repeat) outperforms implicit/incidental exposure for at-risk/dyslexic L2 learners ([International Dyslexia Association guidance](https://dyslexiaida.org/at-risk-students-and-the-study-of-foreign-language-in-school/); Dutch EFL contrastive spelling intervention showing faster, more generalizable gains than regular implicit instruction, [doi:10.1080/09658416.2021.1949332](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658416.2021.1949332)).

### 1.3 ADHD

**Confidence: MODERATE for general app-engagement patterns (real RCT evidence); WEAK-TO-MODERATE for L2-specific findings (real but small, qualitative/self-report).**

- **Direct L2-specific peer-reviewed research exists** — this is a better-populated intersection than expected, from a 2024 Norwegian research program:
  - A quantitative self-report study (91 multilingual adults with ADHD vs. 88 without) found adults with ADHD reported significantly more pragmatic-language difficulty across their L1/L2/L3 than controls, as predicted ([*Clinical Linguistics & Phonetics*, doi:10.1080/02699206.2024.2374909](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699206.2024.2374909)).
  - A companion qualitative study (59 adults with ADHD, free-text responses) identified three themes: (1) cognition (attention, working memory) affects L2 learning/use; (2) different language components/modalities are differentially affected; (3) participants develop individualized compensatory strategies. The headline finding was **heterogeneity**, not a single ADHD-language-learner profile — "many respondents embrace their neurodiversity and develop individual strategies to enhance L2 proficiency" ([doi:10.1016/j.amper.2024.100191](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amper.2024.100191)).
- **General ADHD app-engagement evidence (not language-specific, but relevant to any coaching app):**
  - An 8-week RCT (n=80 children with ADHD) found a gamified educational app (immediate feedback, rewards, level-based challenges) produced significantly larger gains in visual/auditory reaction time, sustained attention, *and* academic scores than a matched non-gamified digital program, with effects largely maintained at follow-up ([Frontiers in Education, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1668260/full)).
  - An adult ADHD CBT-app feasibility study (Inflow, n=240) found median use of 3.86×/week over 7 weeks, with real but moderate engagement (median 27 app opens, 1 module completed, 12 exercises played over the study period) ([PLOS Digital Health, doi:10.1371/journal.pdig.0000083](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pdig.0000083)).
  - A real-world usage audit of an adult ADHD psychoeducation app (~12,000 active users over one year) found average total engaged-session time of only 8 minutes 58 seconds across the whole tracked period, with engagement peaking in the first two weeks post-download and then declining ([doi:10.1016/j.invent.2025.100807](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.invent.2025.100807)) — consistent with a "novelty fades quickly" pattern.
- **Practitioner-level design recommendations (short/variable sessions, active production over passive drilling, avoiding rigid streak mechanics) are directionally consistent with the gamification-attention RCT above, but come from non-peer-reviewed practitioner/blog sources, not a controlled study of language-app design for ADHD specifically** — labeled **assumption — partially unverified** beyond the general engagement-RCT finding.

### 1.4 Autism Spectrum

**Confidence: MODERATE for prosody production; WEAK/MIXED for prosody perception; the specific autism+L2-intonation intersection is essentially unstudied.**

- **Prosody production is the most robustly documented autism-related speech difference.** Autistic speakers show significantly greater pitch range, mean pitch, and maximum pitch than non-autistic controls in both statements and questions, atypical or inappropriate pitch-accent placement, and difficulty producing expected boundary tones (Filipe et al. 2014; Fosnot & Jun 1999; Fusaroli et al. 2017 — all reviewed in [*Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders*, doi:10.1007/s10803-021-05220-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-021-05220-4)). Independent adult raters judge autistic speakers' statement/question productions as less "normal or natural" than controls' — a real social/listener-perception cost, not just an acoustic curiosity.
- **Perception tells a different, more encouraging story.** The same 2021 study (n=84, children through adults) found the *ability* to discriminate, identify, and imitate statement-vs-question intonation was intact in autistic participants across all age cohorts — autistic speakers can typically hear/recognize a prosodic contrast they don't reliably produce. A 2024 study found autistic adolescents *can* perceptually adapt to a new speaker's idiosyncratic prosody after exposure, though possibly to a lesser degree than non-autistic peers, with genuinely mixed findings across the receptive-prosody literature more broadly, partly attributed to population heterogeneity and ceiling effects in some tasks ([*Scientific Reports*, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-66569-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-66569-x)).
- **Tonal-language evidence:** Cantonese-speaking autistic adults show higher pitch (f0) variability than neurotypical peers and less distinct acoustic marking of prosodic "focus" (which syllable signals new/important information) — a pattern also documented in non-tonal languages ([*npj Science of Learning*, doi:10.1038/s41539-026-00425-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-026-00425-7)).
- **Direct evidence for the user's specific concern — risk of penalizing atypical-but-not-wrong prosody:** A systematic review of prosody interventions for Level 1 autism explicitly raises an *ecological-validity* objection to training "generalized imitation of appropriate intonation": what counts as appropriate is context-dependent (e.g., lowering volume to share a secret is appropriate; matching an excited, rapid conversational partner's tone in another context could be read as mocking) — meaning a single scored "target" prosody contour is not just clinically insufficient but can be actively situationally wrong ([PMC8789987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789987/)).
- **No direct research found on "autism and L2 English intonation training" as a combined intersection.** Notably, the same Level 1 Autism prosody review explicitly suggests autism researchers "look to innovative methodologies designed to change the prosody behavior of other populations (e.g., second-language learners; Niebuhr et al., 2017)" as a promising but unexplored direction ([PMC8789987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789987/)) — the literature itself flags this bridge as worth building, but as of the reviewed literature, it has not actually been built or studied.
- **Masking is a documented, independent harm pathway directly relevant to any "correct the intonation" feature.** Autistic "masking" — suppressing or altering natural communication style, including intonation, to match neurotypical expectations — is linked in the literature to increased anxiety, burnout, PTSD-like symptoms, depression, and self-doubt ([Therapist Neurodiversity Collective](https://therapistndc.org/therapy/social-skills-training/); [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/about-autism/autism-and-communication)). ASHA's own Autism Practice Portal has adopted a "neurodiversity-affirming," social-model-of-disability, strengths-based framework, explicitly cautioning against goals aimed at making an autistic person "act more like" non-autistic peers ([ASHA Autism Practice Portal](https://www.asha.org/Practice-Portal/Clinical-Topics/Autism/)). A pronunciation/intonation-scoring feature that implicitly treats one neurotypical-modeled contour as ground truth shares its mechanism with the masking-inducing interventions the field is now moving away from.
- **Gestalt Language Processing (GLP):** many autistic people acquire language in whole memorized chunks/scripts rather than word-by-word, with echolalia — including cross-accent script-copying, per one first-person account ("when we mix and match phrases to form a sentence, we also mix and match accents") — treated as normal, meaningful communication rather than something to suppress ([ASHA Perspectives, doi:10.1044/2023_PERSP-23-00098](https://doi.org/10.1044/2023_PERSP-23-00098); [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/gestalt-language-processing)). **No research was found applying GLP theory to autistic adults' *second*-language acquisition specifically** — an explicit gap — but the general finding that autistic language development can legitimately be non-analytic/chunk-based is directly relevant to any app assuming a single "normal" sounds→words→sentences learning path.

### 1.5 Deaf / Hard of Hearing

**Confidence: MODERATE for the general visual-feedback speech-training literature; WEAK for the specific second-language-acquisition angle.**

- **Population scale:** WHO estimates >1.5 billion people globally live with some degree of hearing loss; 430 million (~5% of the world population) have disabling hearing loss (>35dB in the better ear), projected to exceed 700 million by 2050 ([WHO fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss); [Global Burden of Disease 2021, doi:10.1080/07853890.2025.2527367](https://doi.org/10.1080/07853890.2025.2527367)). ~15% of US adults (37.5 million) report trouble hearing; >25% of adults over 60 have disabling hearing loss ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss); [Audibel/NIH-sourced summary](https://www.audibel.com/hearing-loss-treatment/hearing-loss-statistics-usa/)).
- **Core structural mismatch:** an app whose entire feedback loop is "speak into the microphone, get audio-based feedback" is not merely suboptimal but structurally inaccessible for this population, since — per the deafness/hearing-loss literature — the deaf brain cannot process auditory feedback of its own speech at all ([Journal of Otology, doi:10.26599/JOTO.2025.9540003](https://doi.org/10.26599/joto.2025.9540003)).
- **Existing solutions are visual-feedback-first ("sensory substitution"):**
  - A peer-reviewed pilot (2025) tested a smartphone app converting speech sounds into color-coded "Visual Equivalents" (spectral/temporal patterns compared against a reference target) with 72 deaf children/adults in Delhi schools over 6 months; participants progressed from zero vocalizations to an average of ~7 phonetic sounds (up to 18 for some) — a real, if small-N and uncontrolled, signal that visual sensory-substitution can bootstrap speech production with no auditory feedback at all ([Journal of Otology, doi:10.26599/JOTO.2025.9540003](https://doi.org/10.26599/joto.2025.9540003); [EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1082091)).
  - A parallel peer-reviewed system ("speechcomparison," built at a university that explicitly serves deaf/HoH students) displays real-time waveforms/spectrograms of a student's own speech alongside a reference, with database-backed progress tracking; the paper notes it still needs long-term usability evaluation ([*Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*, doi:10.1121/10.0041482](https://doi.org/10.1121/10.0041482)).
  - Several existing small/hackathon-stage consumer apps (SpeechPath, Ducky AI, SIPHI) already implement live-camera lip-movement guidance and phoneme-to-viseme (mouth-shape) animation specifically for deaf users learning spoken-English pronunciation, demonstrating "visual-feedback-first" coaching is an active, technically-demonstrated design pattern. **Their efficacy claims are unverified** — these are student/hackathon projects with no published outcome data, not clinically validated tools.
- **No research was found specifically on deaf/HoH adults' pronunciation training in the context of *second*-language (as opposed to first spoken-language) acquisition.** The visual-feedback literature above is about deaf individuals learning spoken language generally (often their environment's dominant language), not a documented population of, e.g., signing-deaf adults learning L2 spoken English specifically — a real, citable gap.

## 2. Documented Problems / Design Risks

1. **ASR/scoring bias against stuttered and generally disfluent speech.** A 2024 study evaluating six leading ASR systems found "a consistent and statistically significant accuracy bias... against disfluent speech," with significant syntactic/semantic transcription errors, affecting an estimated 80 million people who stutter globally ([ACL Anthology, 2024.naacl-long.269](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.269/)). Combined with the base literature review's independently-found ASR bias against L2/tonal-L1 speakers, a user who is *both* a person who stutters *and* a non-native speaker could face a compounded misrecognition problem before any coaching logic even runs.
2. **ASR/scoring bias against atypical/disordered speech generally.** Word error rates for dysarthric speech commonly exceed 30–50% even after fine-tuning, and exceed 49% for severe cases across all tested commercial and open systems ([arXiv:2512.17474](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2512.17474); [arXiv:2505.20477](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20477v1); [doi:10.21427/5r8h-px25](https://doi.org/10.21427/5r8h-px25)) — relevant to any user with a motor-speech difference beyond stuttering.
3. **Single-target intonation scoring risks penalizing clinically-normal autistic prosody as "wrong."** Autistic speakers produce measurably different pitch range, contour, and focus-marking than neurotypical speakers as a matter of course ([doi:10.1007/s10803-021-05220-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-021-05220-4); [doi:10.1038/s41539-026-00425-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-026-00425-7)), and the same population's own prosody-intervention literature warns that "matching" a target prosody is context-dependent and can be actively wrong in a different context ([PMC8789987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789987/)) — there may be no single correct target contour to score against, independent of the neurotype question.
4. **Masking-style harm pathway.** If pronunciation/intonation coaching implicitly frames one neurotypical-modeled contour as "correct," this mirrors the masking dynamic already documented to cause anxiety, burnout, depression, and self-doubt in autistic people in other intervention contexts (social-skills training) ([Therapist Neurodiversity Collective](https://therapistndc.org/therapy/social-skills-training/); [National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/about-autism/autism-and-communication)) — this is a documented mechanism, not a hypothetical one.
5. **A fundamentally audio-only feedback loop structurally excludes deaf and profoundly hard-of-hearing users**, not merely under-serves them, since the deaf brain cannot process auditory feedback of its own speech at all ([doi:10.26599/joto.2025.9540003](https://doi.org/10.26599/joto.2025.9540003)).
6. **A real-time/spontaneous-speaking format may disproportionately burden dyslexic users specifically.** The "language spontaneity deficit hypothesis" finding (oral-production gap larger than written-production gap for dyslexic adults) suggests an app explicitly built around real-time spontaneous speaking could be harder for dyslexic users than a scripted/read-aloud-first path — a risk not automatically solved just by having text on screen ([springermedicine.com summary](https://www.springermedicine.com/dyslexia/language-skills-of-adults-with-dyslexia-in-english-as-a-foreign-/50793780)).
7. **Gamified streak/leaderboard mechanics may be actively counterproductive for some ADHD users**, per practitioner-literature observation that "one missed day frequently triggers permanent abandonment" — though this specific claim is weaker-sourced (practitioner blogs) than the general gamification-attention RCT finding, which itself points the *other* direction (gamification helped ADHD children's attention/engagement in a controlled trial). These two threads are in tension and not yet reconciled by any single study of language-app design for ADHD. **Assumption — partially unverified.**
8. **Applying monolingual disfluency norms to any bilingual speaker (not only those who stutter) risks false-positive fluency-disorder flags.** Bilinguals of all kinds show elevated *typical* disfluency rates that can exceed monolingual clinical thresholds ([PMID 40924645](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40924645/); [biblio.ugent.be](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JZQQDVXWYRPJEFYZ73EGNJRH)) — an algorithm that flags "excess disfluency" using monolingual-derived thresholds risks mislabeling ordinary L2 disfluency as disordered for any L2 user, and more severely for one who also stutters.
9. **Voice-navigation accessibility of the app's own interface is a distinct concern from its speech-coaching feature.** WCAG Success Criterion 2.5.3 (Label in Name) requires visible control text to be contained in the spoken/accessible name so users who navigate an entire app by voice-input software (Dragon, Voice Control, Voice Access) — a plausible strategy for some motor- or speech-disabled users — are not blocked ([W3C, SC 2.5.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/label-in-name.html)).
10. **No clinician-in-the-loop, for a population where self-directed digital tools are explicitly cautioned against.** Only 2 of 10 top clinically-marketed speech-therapy apps had *any* published clinical-effectiveness data ([JMIR mHealth, doi carried in base review](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/10/e18858/)), and practitioner sources caution that speech apps "can accidentally reinforce errors" absent a clinician-set target and cueing plan ([breatheworks.com](https://breatheworks.com/speech-therapy-apps-vs-speech-therapy-benefits-limitations/)) — a general risk already flagged in the base review's Cross-Cutting Finding #3, but with sharper stakes here: for a user with an underlying speech/hearing/neurodevelopmental condition, an unsupervised app might be their *only* point of contact given cost/access barriers to SLP care.

## 3. How Professionals / Existing Frameworks Address This

### Universal Design for Learning (UDL), applied to language-learning technology

- CAST's UDL framework rests on three principles, each mapped to a distinct neurological network: **multiple means of engagement** (the "why," affective network), **multiple means of representation** (the "what," recognition network), and **multiple means of action & expression** (the "how," strategic network) ([University of Illinois Chicago CATE summary of the CAST framework](https://teaching.uic.edu/cate-teaching-guides/inclusive-equity-minded-teaching-practices/universal-design-for-learning-udl/)).
- **UDL applied specifically to language-learning technology/CALL is a genuinely thin literature.** Multiple 2024–2025 papers state explicitly that cross-pollinating UDL and CALL "has not yet been explored in depth," and that prior to one researcher's (Kasch) doctoral work, "only one empirical UDL study of language classrooms exists... and no studies before him in lower-secondary language classrooms" ([pure.au.dk, PDF](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/218935391/Innovative_inclusive_educational_technology_in_language_classrooms_and_learner_perspectives_A_study_of_nine_learner_narratives_AM.pdf)). Kasch's UDL-CALL design used five concrete digital scaffolds — multimodal bilingual glossing, contiguous/words-in-context glossing, text-to-speech with highlighting, bilingual retelling functions, and learner-response functions — validated via semi-structured interviews with 9 learners, not an RCT.
- A 2025 CALL-diversity paper confirms UDL's growing relevance to inclusive language-education research but focuses on teacher-trainer *attitudes* toward diversity/inclusion, not validated app-design outcomes ([doi:10.54855/callej.252622](https://doi.org/10.54855/callej.252622)).
- An ACTFL-aligned classroom-UDL paper frames UDL for language classrooms around teacher-designed choice/voice (e.g., choice of writing prompt, audio vs. independent reading) rather than app/technology design specifically ([ERIC EJ1486668](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1486668.pdf)) — the UDL-for-language-learning evidence base is overwhelmingly about classroom teaching, not self-directed app design, a real translation gap for this specific product category.

### Accessibility guidelines specific to voice/speech-based apps

- **WCAG 2.2** ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag/)) includes provisions directly relevant to a voice-based app: **SC 1.2.1** (Audio-only and Video-only, Prerecorded) requires a text alternative for audio-only content; **SC 1.2.9** (Audio-only, Live) extends this to live audio; **SC 1.4.7** (Low or No Background Audio) requires foreground speech to be free of, or reducible/replaceable relative to, background sound. **SC 2.5.3** (Label in Name, Level A) requires a control's visible text label to be contained in its accessible/spoken name specifically so voice-input users (Dragon, Voice Control, Voice Access) can navigate by speaking on-screen labels ([W3C, SC 2.5.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/label-in-name.html)) — relevant to the app's *own interface*, distinct from its speech-coaching feature.
- **W3C's Cognitive and Learning Disabilities Accessibility Task Force (COGA)** publishes voice/conversational-interface-specific guidance explicitly naming ADHD, autism, dyslexia, and other cognitive disabilities as target populations ([Voice Systems and Conversational Interfaces](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-voice/); [Natural Language Interface Accessibility User Requirements](https://www.w3.org/TR/naur/)), with concrete recommendations directly applicable to a speech-coaching app:
  - Speech recognition "should be trained and tested with diverse user groups... for users with non-typical speech patterns or speech impairments," with explicit "contingencies for pauses, use of incorrect terms, and mistakes."
  - Always provide a non-voice (text/menu) fallback path, since natural-language voice-only interfaces "can be difficult to use for users who have difficulty producing speech or language."
  - Use simple, present-tense, active-voice sentence structure and chunked instructions, which "benefits many people such as people with language impairments, dyslexia, or a memory impairment" ([Design Guide for Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html)) — notably, this is the *same* simplification guidance that also benefits L2 learners generally, a rare point of natural alignment between accessibility guidance and this app's core audience.

### ASHA / clinical accommodation practice

- ASHA's **Accent Modification Practice Portal** frames SLP-delivered accent-modification/pronunciation coaching as an elective, non-disorder service requiring client-centered, culturally-responsive delivery, and explicitly acknowledges internal field criticism that such services can "perpetuate stigma... rather than embracing diversity of language" ([ASHA Practice Portal](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/professional-issues/accent-modification/)) — the same tension independently flagged in the base literature review's Cross-Cutting Finding #2.
- ASHA's **Autism Practice Portal** has separately adopted a "neurodiversity-affirming," social-model-of-disability, strengths-based framework, citing a published clinician flowchart (Crow, 2024) and explicitly warning against treatment goals aimed at making an autistic person "act more like" non-autistic peers ([ASHA Autism Practice Portal](https://www.asha.org/Practice-Portal/Clinical-Topics/Autism/)).
- **No ASHA-level (or equivalent professional-body) guidance was found combining "elective accent/pronunciation modification services" with "neurodiversity-affirming care" as a single named practice document** — the two exist as separate practice portals. Independent, non-ASHA "neurodiversity-affirming speech therapy" clinics describe applying the same client-autonomy, strengths-based, environmental-modification principles to accent-related work ([talkshop.com.au](https://www.talkshop.com.au/neurodiverse-affirming-speech-therapy-what-it-is-and-why-it-matters/); [speechaus.com](https://speechaus.com/neurodiversity-affirming-speech-therapy/)), but these are single-clinic sources, not ASHA-level clinical consensus. Labeled accordingly: real-but-limited authority.
- For bilingual stuttering specifically, treatment-language flexibility (either language, simultaneous or sequential) plus continued encouragement of the home language are the concrete, citable clinical accommodations (see §1.1 above).

## 5. Open Questions / Research Gaps

Stated honestly, per the grounding rule, rather than filled with speculation:

1. **No research was found on autism and L2 English intonation-training as a combined intersection.** The autism-prosody literature itself explicitly names this as an unexplored bridge worth building (citing second-language-learner methodology as a model to borrow from) but has not, as far as this research found, actually studied it ([PMC8789987](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8789987/)).
2. **No research was found on deaf/HoH adults' visual-feedback speech training specifically in the context of *second* (rather than first-encountered) spoken-language acquisition.** The visual-feedback/sensory-substitution literature is entirely about deaf individuals learning spoken language in general, not a documented L1-signing-adult-learning-L2-spoken-English population.
3. **No research was found applying Gestalt Language Processing theory to autistic adults' L2 (as opposed to L1) acquisition.**
4. **The UDL-for-app-based/self-directed-CALL evidence base is essentially one PhD research program (Kasch)** — thin even by the standards of an emerging field, and entirely classroom-based rather than tested on self-directed mobile-app usage.
5. **No ASHA-level (or equivalent professional-body) guidance was found combining "elective accent/pronunciation modification" with "neurodiversity-affirming care" as a single practice document** — they exist as two separate, non-overlapping practice portals.
6. **No study was found directly testing whether ASR-based, real-time L2-pronunciation-coaching apps built for the general L2 population are safe or effective for L2 learners who are *also* stuttering, autistic, dyslexic, or D/HoH.** Every located study tested either (a) general-population L2 pronunciation apps on unspecified-neurotype users, or (b) accessibility-specific speech apps (deaf visual feedback, stuttering apps) on monolingual or unspecified-L1 users. The specific intersection this document was asked to research — an accessible-design coaching app *for L2 learners who are also neurodivergent/D-HoH/stutter* — appears to be genuinely unstudied as a combined population.
7. **The bilingual-stuttering mechanism itself remains disputed in the primary literature** — higher L2 disfluency is the majority finding, but conflicting studies exist with no agreed mechanistic explanation for the reverse pattern.
8. **The specific "short sessions, no streaks, active production" ADHD-app-design recommendations common in practitioner sources remain experimentally untested as applied to language-learning apps for ADHD users specifically** — the peer-reviewed ADHD-app-engagement evidence base tests general educational/CBT apps, not language-coaching apps, and one RCT finding (gamification helped attention) sits in unresolved tension with the practitioner-sourced caution against streak mechanics.

## 6. Confidence in the Evidence Base

**Overall: MODERATE, and unevenly distributed across conditions.** The underlying clinical/epidemiological literature for each individual condition — bilingual stuttering patterns, dyslexia's phonological mechanism, ADHD prevalence, autism prosody production, hearing-loss epidemiology — is well-established, often via peer-reviewed studies, systematic reviews, or large-scale government surveillance data (CDC BRFSS, WHO, NCHS MMWR). What is **weak-to-absent** across almost every condition is the *specific bridge* this document was asked to research: how an L2-speaking coaching app specifically should accommodate each condition. Most citable findings describe the condition, or describe L2 acquisition, as separate literatures; the applied intersection is mostly unstudied, and any design conclusion drawn from this document would need to be explicitly flagged as an *inference* from adjacent evidence, not a directly-tested finding.

- **Strongest evidence base: Stuttering.** Bilingual stuttering is an actual, dedicated sub-field within fluency-disorders research, with systematic reviews, a 2024/2025 recalculated meta-analytic prevalence figure, cross-linguistic generalization studies, and explicit ASHA-affiliated clinical guidance for bilingual treatment. **Prevalence data generally** is also strong, resting on large government surveillance systems (CDC BRFSS, WHO, NCHS).
- **Weakest evidence base: Autism + L2-intonation intersection and UDL-for-app-based-CALL.** The autism-prosody-and-L2 bridge is explicitly named as unexplored by the autism literature itself; the UDL-CALL literature consists of essentially one PhD research program. Deaf/HoH + L2-specifically is also very weak: the visual-feedback speech-training research is real and peer-reviewed, but was never conducted in an explicit second-language-acquisition frame.
