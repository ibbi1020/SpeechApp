# Scoping — the entry ritual (home → exercise start)

**Scope:** only the flow from the home screen to the moment an exercise begins. Nothing inside a
session. Nothing after a report.

**Status:** research pass. No decision made. Written to answer one question: is adding steps between
home and a session a cost to minimise, or a mechanism that makes the session worth more?

**Short answer:** it is a mechanism, and this app has an unusually strong claim on it — but only
under four boundary conditions the literature is specific about, and only if the added steps do not
raise abandonment. Both directions are evidenced. Neither is a matter of taste.

---

## 1. The claim being tested

> Reducing the steps from home to starting an exercise creates an easy-to-access app. That portrays
> it as low value. Users who did not invest getting in will not spend willpower staying in.

The literature supports a version of this claim, contradicts the strongest version of it, and —
importantly — offers a better reason to slow the entry down than perceived value.

---

## 2. Evidence that effort and ceremony raise value

**Self-made things are valued more (IKEA effect).** Across assembling IKEA boxes, folding origami,
and building Lego, people valued their own output as highly as an expert's, and expected others to
agree ([Norton, Mochon & Ariely 2012, *J. Consumer Psychology* 22(3):453–460,
doi:10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2011.08.002)). A meta-analysis of 55
studies (N = 5,454) puts the effect on valuation at **d = 0.57**, robust across customisation and
tangibility, with knock-on effects on liking and sense of ownership
([Labor Leads to Love, Right? A Meta-Analysis of the IKEA
Effect](https://exa.ai/library/publication/k7m0qz2g1kk) — abstract read; journal of record not
independently confirmed).

**The mechanism is felt competence, not sunk cost.** Feelings of competence mediate the increased
valuation; affirming someone's self beforehand *reduces* the effect, and threatening it *increases*
their appetite to make the thing themselves ([Mochon, Norton & Ariely 2012, *Int. J. of Research in
Marketing*, doi:10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.05.001) —
abstract read). This matters here: our user's competence in spoken English is exactly what feels
threatened. That is the population the effect is *strongest* in.

**Visible effort raises perceived value even when it changes nothing (labor illusion).** Replacing a
progress bar with a running tally of work being done made people prefer *longer* waits for
*identical* results, across five experiments. Mediated by perceived provider effort and a feeling of
reciprocity ([Buell & Norton 2011, *Management Science* 57(9):1564–1579,
doi:10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376)).

**Ritual before an anxious performance reduces anxiety and improves the performance.** This is the
strongest finding for this app specifically. A predefined sequence of symbolic actions, performed
before public singing and before maths testing, lowered self-reported anxiety *and* heart rate, and
improved performance. Rituals beat both distraction and "try to calm down." Critically, **the same
actions worked better when called a "ritual" than when called "random behaviors"**
([Brooks, Schroeder, Risen, Gino, Galinsky, Norton & Schweitzer 2016, *OBHDP* 137:71–85,
doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2016.07.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2016.07.004)).

Our three exercises are all anxious performances. Foreign Language Anxiety compounding
public-speaking anxiety is already a documented barrier in `research/literature-review.md`. A
pre-session ritual is therefore not decoration bolted onto a timer — it is a cited intervention
against the thing that stops our user from speaking.

**Ritual raises the enjoyment of what follows, and a delay raises it further.** Four experiments
across chocolate, lemonade and carrots: ritualised gestures beat random gestures; performing the
ritual yourself beat watching someone else perform it; and **inserting a delay between the ritual and
the consumption produced the highest enjoyment of all**. The mechanism is involvement
([Vohs, Wang, Gino & Norton 2013, *Psychological Science* 24(9):1714–1721,
doi:10.1177/0956797613478949](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613478949)).

**A small deliberate obstacle switches people from automatic to deliberate.** "Microboundaries" —
small, easily overcome obstacles placed before an interaction — create a pause for reflection and
move behaviour from System 1 to System 2. The paper is explicit that *long* lockouts backfire into
task-switching ([Cox, Gould, Cecchinato, Iacovides & Renfree 2016, CHI EA '16:1389–1397,
doi:10.1145/2851581.2892410](https://doi.org/10.1145/2851581.2892410)).

**Naming the plan is the single best-evidenced step we could add.** Specifying the when, where and
how of an intention in advance raises goal attainment by **d = 0.65** across 94 independent tests —
**d = 0.61** for getting started and **d = 0.77** for not derailing once underway
([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38:69–119,
doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)). FocusFlight's
"What do you want to focus?" chip is this, whether or not its designers knew.

## 3. Evidence against — the boundary conditions

These are not caveats. They are the conditions under which the effect **reverses**.

1. **Labor only leads to love when the labor completes.** Participants who built and then destroyed
   their creations, or who failed to finish, showed **no** IKEA effect (Norton et al. 2012). A ritual
   that makes someone abandon before the session starts is worse than no ritual. It produces the
   negative-affect-and-regret pattern of an unfinished task, not value.
2. **Friction measurably raises drop-off.** In an FCA online investment experiment, an added
   manual-input step made people **6.2 percentage points** less likely to finish, and a
   personalisation step **4 pp**; each input error cost a further 3.1 pp
   ([FCA, *Pausing, reading, and reflecting: decision points in high-risk investment consumer
   journeys*](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/research/decision-points-consumer-journeys.pdf)).
   The same paper endorses microboundaries. Both things are true: the pause helps the people who get
   through it, and fewer people get through it.
3. **It must be a ritual, and it must be theirs.** Random gestures underperform systematic ones
   (Vohs et al. 2013). Watching underperforms doing (same). Actions framed as "random behaviors"
   underperform the identical actions framed as a ritual (Brooks et al. 2016). Arbitrary taps that
   read as busywork get none of this.
4. **Our own accessibility review constrains session length and attention, hard.** A real-world audit
   of ~12,000 adult ADHD app users found average total engaged time of **8 minutes 58 seconds across
   an entire year**, peaking in the first two weeks ([doi:10.1016/j.invent.2025.100807]
   (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.invent.2025.100807), via
   `research/accessibility-neurodivergence-review.md`). A long entry ceremony spends a budget we do
   not have. Note the review's own honesty flag: the "short sessions" design recommendation is
   practitioner-sourced, partially unverified.

What the recording shows that is worth stealing:

- **Almost nothing is actually arbitrary.** One genuinely required parameter (duration), one
  evidence-backed planning step (what you're focusing on), one real commitment device (airplane

- **The last step is a pause, not an action.** "Cabin doors closed, Ready for takeoff" does nothing
  functional. It is exactly the post-ritual delay that produced the highest enjoyment in Vohs et al.

## 5. The other problem: users will default to the easiest exercise

This is a separate, better-evidenced worry than perceived value, and the evidence is blunt.

**People avoid cognitive demand even when they cannot tell they are doing it.** Across six
experiments people reliably chose the less demanding option; the bias did not depend on awareness of
the manipulation, was not explained by error-avoidance or saving time, and **covaried with executive
control** ([Kool, McGuire, Rosen & Botvinick 2010, *JEP: General* 139(4):665–682,
doi:10.1037/a0020198](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020198)). Low executive control is a defining feature
of part of our target population.

**Learners actively prefer the practice that teaches them less, and keep preferring it after being
shown their own results.** Interleaving beat blocking at learning painters' styles, yet **about 80%
of participants judged blocking to be as good or better — after their own test had proved otherwise**
([Kornell & Bjork 2008, *Psychological Science*,
doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)). Related:
interleaved practice of volume formulas produced **63% vs 20%** correct a week later
([Rohrer & Taylor 2007, via Bjork & Bjork
2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)).
Attempts to correct the illusion by telling people the truth mostly failed: even when told that 90%
of people learn better with interleaving *and* that fluency is misleading, a majority still rated
blocking as good or better ([Yan, Bjork & Bjork 2016, *JEP: General*,
doi:10.1037/xge0000177](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000177)).

Applied here, with the labels this project already uses: Reading is the fluent, low-demand,
feels-productive option. Conversation is the high-demand one and the one `docs/product-spec.md` names
as the target behaviour. Left to free choice with equal access, the literature predicts drift toward
Reading **and** confident self-reports that Reading is helping most. Telling users otherwise is
documented to mostly not work.

**Inference, not a finding:** none of this was tested on speaking practice, exercise pickers, or
L2 learners. The transfer is ours, and it is an inference.

## 6. Principles

Candidate rules for the home → start flow. Each is traceable to §2–§5.

1. **Ceremony is justified here by anxiety, not by perceived value.** Lead on Brooks et al. 2016.
   Pre-performance ritual is a cited intervention for the barrier this app exists to remove.
2. **Dress real steps; do not invent fake ones.** FocusFlight's steps are mostly a required
   parameter, a plan, and a commitment device in costume. One piece of pure theatre out of four.
3. **Name the plan.** One step where the user says what this session is for is the best-evidenced
   addition available (d = 0.65 / 0.61 / 0.77). It is also a step, so it is not free.
4. **Express parameters as consequences.** Drag until you reach the city, do not type the minutes.
5. **No keepsake artifact before the session.** The IKEA effect needs a completed object, but the
   object is the output of the *labor* — and the labor here is the session, not the entry. That
   object already exists: the report, and the saved recording. FocusFlight's boarding pass is not an
   IKEA artifact, it is its **delay device**, the beat between ritual and start — and we already own
   that beat (`I'm ready` + 3-2-1). What the entry flow does need is for the answers to stay
   visible as they accumulate, so the last screen shows the whole set. That is completeness without
   a second keepsake.
6. **End on a held beat, not an action.** A short deliberate pause before the session starts is the
   condition that produced the largest effect in Vohs et al. 2013.
7. **The primary button stays put and changes its label.** Already specified; it is also what makes
   extra steps feel like one continuous act rather than a queue of screens.
8. **Call it what it is.** The symbolic framing is load-bearing, not flavour text. Actions labelled
   "random" measurably underperform the same actions labelled as ritual.
9. **The ritual must be short enough to always finish.** An abandoned ritual produces regret, not
   value. Completion is the boundary condition on the entire §2 case.
10. **Do not let the cheapest exercise become the default by being the cheapest to reach.** Effort
    avoidance operates below awareness. Equal access is not neutral; it favours the easy option.
11. **Do not try to fix the easy-option bias by explaining it.** Telling learners that the harder
    practice works better is documented to mostly fail. Structure beats persuasion.
12. **A showed-up / missed-days board is out of scope and contested.** `docs/product-spec.md` puts
    progress chips and daily pushes at "later, not v1" and sets the habit metric at conversations per
    *week*, not per day. `research/accessibility-neurodivergence-review.md` flags that streak
    mechanics may be actively counterproductive for some ADHD users ("one missed day frequently
    triggers permanent abandonment") — weakly sourced, and in unresolved tension with a gamification
    RCT that points the other way. A board that counts **missed** days is the riskiest form of it.
13. **Measure abandonment before and after, per exercise.** The entire §2 case is conditional on
    completion, and §3.2 gives a concrete expectation of the cost (single-digit percentage points).

## 7. Candidate ceremony steps, ranked by evidence

The ritual has to be evidence-backed, not just atmospheric. Here is what the literature actually
supports, strongest first. Two popular candidates do **not** survive the check.

### Tier 1 — well evidenced

**Name what this session is for.** An implementation intention: the when, where and how, stated in
advance. **d = 0.65** across 94 tests, **d = 0.61** for getting started
([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)). This is the single
best-evidenced step available, and it is what FocusFlight's "What do you want to focus?" chip is.

*Can the app state the purpose instead of the user?* Mostly yes, under one condition. In a
four-arm randomised trial (n = 248), experimenter-provided and self-generated implementation
intentions produced statistically indistinguishable results (p = .62), and **"simply choosing an
experimenter-provided implementation intention was just as effective as writing it out in full"**
([Armitage & Arden 2009?, *Health Psychology*,
PMID 19751080](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751080/) — abstract read; year to confirm). Two
caveats: a sustainable-behaviour meta-analysis found **personalised plans (d = 0.578) beat prompted
plans (d = 0.241), p = 0.010**, and third-party revision of someone's plan *lowered* effectiveness
by lowering commitment. Self-generated plans also fail when the plans are vague — in one trial over
20% of participants did not complete the self-generated version properly.

**Design consequence:** an app-stated purpose keeps the effect only if the user **adopts** it with an
act — a tap that accepts, or better, a small choice between stated purposes. A line of copy they
merely scroll past is not an implementation intention. The ceiling is lower than a self-written plan.

**A vocal warm-up.** This is the step that is genuinely real for a speech app, and it has direct
evidence:
- A six-week RCT in teachers: the vocal warm-up group reduced Voice Handicap Index and lowered mean
  F0, and reported **general voice improvement (p = 0.003) and greater ease of speech (p = 0.030)
  significantly more often than the breathing-training group** ([Pereira et al. 2015, *Rev. Saúde
  Pública* 49:67](https://www.scielosp.org/article/rsp/2015.v49/67/en/)).
- Immediate effects exist at short doses: 20 minutes of warm-up significantly reduced jitter and
  shimmer in non-singers ([*Turkish J. ENT*
  2024](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/trent/article/1454224)); a **1-minute** water-resistance
  exercise produced significant immediate acoustic change per repetition ([*J. Voice*
  2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2021.09.009) — abstract read).

Note the head-to-head: when warm-up and breathing training were compared directly, warm-up won on
ease of speech. For this app a warm-up also doubles as the lowest-stakes vocalisation of the session
— the user is already making sound before the exercise starts.

**Paced breathing, at 6 breaths a minute.** Reinstated by decision, and the pacing numbers are not
guesswork even though the anxiety effect is weak:
- The adult resonance-frequency range is **4.5 to 6.5 breaths per minute** (Lehrer & Gevirtz 2014),
  so a **10-second cycle** is the standard target.
- The conventional split is **4 seconds in, 6 seconds out**, used in both the dose-response study and
  the anxiety RCT below. But the ratio is **not** proven: an original study (N = 26) and its
  replication (N = 16) found a 1:2 ratio produced **no difference** in time-domain, frequency-domain
  or nonlinear HRV versus 1:1 at 6 bpm ([Appl. Psychophysiol. Biofeedback 2024,
  doi:10.1007/s10484-024-09637-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-024-09637-2)). Use 4 + 6 because it
  is the common protocol, not because longer exhalation is established.
- **Dose: 5 minutes is the tested floor and longer adds nothing.** N = 59, within-subject, at 5, 10,
  15 and 20 minutes: every condition raised RMSSD over control, with **no differences between
  durations** ([Laborde et al. 2021, PMC8656666](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8656666/)).
  A just-in-time anxiety RCT used **2-minute** sessions when symptoms rose.
- **Do not instruct a deep breath during this step.** Both protocols include explicit
  anti-hyperventilation instruction; forcing depth is the documented failure mode. The breath cue
  belongs to the vocal step, where it is breath support for phonation, not an anxiety technique.

Three cycles is 30 seconds, which is a quarter of the 2-minute just-in-time dose and a tenth of
the 5-minute floor. Flag as inference: the pacing is evidenced, the abbreviated length is not.

**Rotating the vocal exercise is justified, not just variety.** Humming is one of several
semi-occluded vocal tract exercises and is not the best of them:
- An electroglottographic study of seven SOVT exercises in 23 volunteers splits them into two
  physiologically distinct families: **steady, single-source** (hand-over-mouth, humming, straw) with
  lower contact-quotient range, and **fluctuating, dual-source** (tongue trill, lip trill, LaxVox)
  with larger range. The authors state **different therapeutic effects can be expected** from the two
  groups ([*J. Voice*, doi:10.1016/j.jvoice.2013.05.007](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(13)00240-3/abstract)).
  Rotating across both families is therefore defensible on mechanism, not only on boredom.
- A four-arm RCT in dysphonia (44 participants) found lip trill, straw phonation and vocal function
  exercises all beat no treatment, and the **lip trill group showed the best improvements** of the
  three ([*J. Voice* 2025](https://www.jvoice.org/article/S0892-1997(25)00192-4/abstract)).
- A separate RCT comparing tongue trill, lip trill, straw and water-resistance concluded **no single
  SOVT can be definitively recommended** as most effective.
- Resonant voice training on **nasal consonants** improved the dysphonia severity index, while straw
  phonation expanded intensity range, with no between-group difference, in 30 healthy occupational
  voice users over six weeks ([*JSLHR* 2017,
  doi:10.1044/2017_JSLHR-S-17-0017](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/2017_JSLHR-S-17-0017)).

Straw phonation and water-resistance need equipment and are out. That leaves four that can be
demonstrated by audio alone: **hum (/m/)**, **nasal "ng"**, **lip trill**, **tongue trill** — two from
each family.

**Anchor attention in the present.** The goal is not to raise or lower arousal — it is to pull a
divided mind into the room. This target has its own evidence, and it is stronger for *attention* than
for anxiety:
- Acute, brief, audio-based mindfulness inductions in anxious adults produced **state mindfulness
  g = 0.91** (k = 2, n = 110, CI [0.52, 1.30], p < .001) and state anxiety g = −0.60 (k = 3, n = 100,
  CI [−1.04, −0.16], p = .008). The authors' own conclusion is that **gains in state mindfulness are
  the more realistic expected outcome** than anxiety reduction — which is exactly the target here.
  Caveat stated by the authors: few studies, high risk of bias, low certainty.
- Effects appear "after only one session and with interventions as brief as **5 min**"; across 85
  mostly-RCT studies, single-session inductions ran from under 5 to 25 min, **median 10 min**
  ([Howarth et al. 2019, *Mindfulness*,
  doi:10.1007/s12671-019-01163-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-019-01163-1)).
- **Critically, duration is not a moderator.** One session g = .22 vs longer g = .12, not
  significant; meta-regressions on minutes, sessions and days were all null ([Schumer, Lindsay &
  Creswell 2018, *J. Consult. Clin. Psych.*,
  PMC6441958](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6441958/)). This is the opposite of PMR, where
  the dose was the whole problem — so a short attentional step is not obviously underdosed.
- **Honest counterweight from the same paper:** for *negative affectivity*, brief mindfulness was
  g = .21, and after adjusting for publication bias **g = .04**. Attention is the defensible claim.
  Mood change is not.

**What practitioners actually prescribe before speaking.** Checked against an NHS speech and
language therapy patient leaflet, an SLP clinic's public-speaking guidance, a Vocal Function
Exercises clinical handout, and a 2024 acoustic study summarising Titze's recommended set. The
sequence is consistent across all of them:
1. **Release physical tension** — shoulders, neck, jaw, tongue. Every source puts this *first*.
2. **Connect the breath** — slow, low breaths; the yawn-sigh.
3. **Wake the voice** — humming on closed lips, lip trills ("brrrr"), pitch glides and sirens.
4. **Wake the articulators** — sustained fricatives, vowel sequences ("mah, may, mee, moh, moo"),
   one slow over-articulated tongue twister.
5. **Say your opening line** out loud at the volume you intend to use.

The mechanism behind step 3 is the **semi-occluded vocal tract** (SOVT) family — lip trills, tongue
trills, humming, nasal consonants, straw phonation, LaxVox, hand-over-mouth, finger kazoo — held to
reduce phonation threshold pressure and perceived phonatory effort. Titze's recommended five lead
with exactly these. Vocal Function Exercises (Stemple; Roy et al. 2001) add upward and downward pitch
glides on "knoll" or "boom," with the goal of no voice breaks across the glide.
Sources: [Royal Berkshire NHS FT voice warm-up
leaflet](https://www.royalberkshire.nhs.uk/media/5ehceiw2/voice-warm-up-and-warm-down_apr24.pdf),
[MedSLP Collective VFE
handout](https://medslpcollective.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/MedSLPCollective-Handout-Vocal-Function-Exercises.pdf),
[Connected Speech
Pathology](https://connectedspeechpathology.com/blog/how-to-improve-vocal-tone-for-public-speaking-and-presenting),
[*Turkish J. ENT* 2024](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3800833).

**This partially rehabilitates the body step — on different grounds.** The body step died as an
*anxiety* intervention (PMR dose, harm signal) and as a *posture* intervention (sit-vs-stand VRP
nulls). Neither finding touches jaw, neck, shoulder and tongue release as **voice preparation**,
which is where practitioner consensus is unanimous and where it is always placed first. The null
posture studies compared sitting with standing; they did not test releasing a clenched jaw.

**Caveat on one source.** A "60s / 5min / 15min" public-speaking warm-up guide surfaced at
`theoratorapp.com`, which appears to be our own site. Not counted as independent evidence, though its
claim that the short version can be done near-silently (jaw release, low breathing, closed-lip hum)
is consistent with the clinical sources above.

**Which practice you pick does not seem to matter — so pick on fit.** 160 adults were randomised to
one of four MBCT practices: body scan, mindful movement, breath and body, and befriending. State-level
effects appeared in every candidate mechanism and outcome, d = 0.27 to 0.86 — but **no between-group
effects were found, p = 0.050 to 0.973** ([Mindfulness 2023,
doi:10.1007/s12671-023-02193-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-023-02193-6)). Four quite different
practices, indistinguishable results. State *decentering* was the one thing that did not move. The
design implication is direct: stop hunting for the technique with the best effect and choose the one
that fits the product and that people will actually do.

**The Three-Minute Breathing Space is the closest named protocol.** Built into MBCT by Segal,
Williams and Teasdale specifically as a structure for "noting, grounding, and allowing — in the midst
of challenging situations or **whenever automatic pilot took over**," which is the stated problem
here. Its shape is three roughly one-minute steps, deliberately alternating the width of attention:
1. **Wide** — attend broadly to what is already present, noting it without needing to change it.
2. **Narrow** — a single pointed focus (in MBCT, the breath in the body).
3. **Wide again** — the body as a whole, and whatever sensations are there.

Evidence when isolated is thin but on-target where it counts: a classroom study (n = 88, scripted
3MBS, under 5 min daily) found **fewer episodes of mind wandering and distractibility** than control
plus greater positive emotionality — and **no detectable effect on mindfulness** or negative
emotionality. Participants reported enjoying it and over half used it outside class. A one-session
GAD trial found reduced current stress but not worry, and CBT outperformed it.

**Mapping onto our ceremony:** the alternation is already the shape we have. A wide attention step,
then the hum as the single pointed focus, then the countdown widening back out. Our narrow step is
sound rather than breath, which suits a voice app and is covered by the no-between-group-differences
result above. Compressing three minutes to ceremony length is **inference** — defensible because
duration was not a moderator, but not a matched dose.

**Heartbeat counting does not qualify, despite being the most interesting candidate.** As an
attention manipulation it is real: interoceptive attention raises the heartbeat-evoked potential
versus exteroceptive attention with no change in heart rate ([Petzschner et al.
2020](https://fpetzschner.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Petzschner-et-al.-2020-Focus-of-attention-modulates-the-heartbeat-evoked-potential.pdf)),
and an fMRI meta-analysis of 9 studies finds an insula-centred network. But it fails on two grounds:
- **Not everyone can do it.** Scores drop by about 50% when participants are told to report only
  beats they actually felt ([Desmedt et al.
  2018](https://www.uclep.be/labs/ARL/publications/Desmedt%20et%20al._2018.pdf)); in N = 572, over
  95% of scores were under-reports and the correlation between actual and reported beats was
  **r = .16** ([Zamariola et al.
  2018](https://sites.uclouvain.be/FOOD4GUT/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Zamariola-et-al.-Biological-Psychology.-2018..pdf)).
- **It points attention at the sensation that drives panic.** Inward-facing techniques are the ones
  that go wrong for people whose anxiety is somatic, which is the same warning attached to PMR above.

**Sensory grounding scripts (5-4-3-2-1) do not qualify.** No trial has tested the sequence. A 2026
critical review found grounding as a *category* has never been subjected to efficacy studies because
the field has no agreed operational definition. The one application of the named method was a
non-randomised quality-improvement project in ICU nurses measuring burnout, with small effects and a
non-significant result. The mechanism it borrows — loading limited attention with concrete external
detail leaves less capacity for internal threat processing — is sound; the script is unvalidated, runs
5–7 minutes, and requires smell and taste. Take the mechanism, not the script.

**Write down the worry about the thing you are about to do.** The strongest single result for a
written pre-task step. Two laboratory and two randomised field experiments; a brief expressive
writing assignment immediately before a high-stakes exam significantly improved scores, **especially
for habitually anxious students**, who beat controls by about 6%, **d = 0.57** ([Ramirez & Beilock
2011, *Science* 331(6014):211–213, doi:10.1126/science.1199427](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199427)).
Three details matter for us:
- **Specificity is the active ingredient.** "It is not any writing that benefits performance, but
  expressing worries about an upcoming high-pressure situation." Writing about the previous day in an
  unemotional, factual manner, and sitting quietly, **both choked** under pressure.
- The effect was expressive writing *raising* scores, not the control condition depressing them — a
  separate group who spent the time studying showed no difference from control (F(1,78) = 0.59,
  p = 0.44).
- **Dose is 10 minutes**, and unlike the mindfulness literature there is no finding here that
  duration fails to moderate. Compressing this to a one-line field is **inference, and a weaker
  inference** than the mindfulness compression.

Against it: the FCA field evidence that each added input step cost **6.2pp** completion, and typing
is the most expensive input there is. A 10-minute write before every session also cannot coexist with
a fixed-length ceremony that runs daily.

**Say "I am excited" out loud — and do not try to calm down.** This is the most domain-relevant
finding in the document, and it contradicts the obvious plan. Across studies on **karaoke singing,
public speaking and maths**, people who reappraised their pre-performance anxiety as *excitement*
outperformed people who tried to calm down. The manipulation is minimal: saying "I am excited" aloud,
or even just being told to "get excited." Mechanism is an opportunity mindset replacing a threat
mindset ([Brooks 2014, *JEP: General* 143(3):1144–1158,
doi:10.1037/a0035325](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035325)).

The pilot study in the same paper found that **an overwhelming majority of people believe calming
down is the best strategy** — so the intuition to "slow the user down" is the common intuition, and
it is the arm that lost. Note this sits alongside Brooks et al. 2016 (rituals reduce anxiety and
help); the two are not in conflict — a ritual can be the vehicle, but its content should reappraise
arousal rather than suppress it.

**A held beat before the start.** A delay between the ritual and the thing itself produced the
*highest* enjoyment in Vohs et al. 2013. **We already have this**: all three formats run `I'm ready`
then a 3-2-1 countdown. Part of the ritual is built.

### Tier 2 — looked strong, does not survive the dose check

**Active embodiment (progressive muscle relaxation).** In the acute state-anxiety review, active
embodiment was the strongest arm: **g = −1.05**, PMR alone at **g = −1.57**. Then the detail:

- The dose in both PMR studies was **20–25 minutes with a live facilitator present** — and that was
  the *abbreviated* protocol (Pawlow & Jones 2002, [doi:10.1016/s0301-0511(02)00010-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0301-0511(02)00010-8);
  2005, [doi:10.1007/s10484-005-8423-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-005-8423-2)). Interventions
  across the whole review ran **11.7–45 min**. There is no evidence for a ten-second version.
- It rests on two studies, I² = 83%, and the 2005 outlier had to be removed for publication bias.
- The same review warns that **"PMR and certain breathing exercises … can in fact intensify
  anxiety"**, especially in panic disorder. That is an active harm signal for an anxious population.

([Hughes et al. 2024, *Front. Psychol.*, PMC11203600](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11203600/))

**Posture as a voice step does not work either.** The plausible-sounding "stand tall for a better
voice" is not supported: in 30 vocally healthy untrained adults, **no voice-range-profile parameter
differed between sitting and standing (all p > 0.05)**, with very small effect sizes
([PMC12456106](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12456106/)). A singer pilot study found no
significant posture effects on muscle activation, aerodynamics, pitch or loudness
([PMC10924224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10924224/)). Only supine and prone differ
measurably from upright, which is not a usable instruction.

**Verdict: drop the body step.** It fails as an anxiety intervention at ceremony length, it fails as
a voice step at any length, and it carries a documented risk of making anxiety worse.

**What the review endorses instead:** for *combined* interventions, "taking a more passive approach
by directing attention towards cognition, body and/or breathing may be more effective than active
change approaches." Passive attention beat active change. Any settling moment we keep should be
attention, not instruction.

**Physical exertion (press-ups, squats) as the body step.** Real evidence, wrong shape for a
ceremony:
- Acute exercise does reduce state anxiety, but the pooled effect is **small: Hedges' g = 0.16**
  across 36 RCTs ([Ensari et al. 2015, *Depression & Anxiety*,
  doi:10.1002/da.22370](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22370)). A larger pre-post meta-analysis of 103
  studies found g = 0.497 for anxiety but with substantial unexplained heterogeneity
  ([PMID 38787545](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38787545/)).
- A 2023 systematic review found only **7 of 13 exercise conditions (53%)** significantly reduced
  anxiety, with **no clear pattern** of mode, duration or intensity
  ([Connor et al. 2023](https://www.tnu.ethz.ch/fileadmin/user_upload/documents/Publications/2023/2023_Connor_Hargreaves_Scanlon_Harrison.pdf)).
- Doses run **10–45 minutes**, and anxiety is typically measured **5–20 minutes after** the bout, not
  immediately. The only short-bout result found is a 1997 conference abstract, n = 36, no control
  group.
- Our own `research/accessibility-neurodivergence-review.md` population includes people for whom a
  physical gate is simply a wall.

**Verdict:** personal experience that press-ups help is consistent with the literature's direction,
but an in-app exertion step has no dose evidence at ceremony length, a 53% hit rate at proper
length, an accessibility floor, and it would add 10+ minutes to a sequence that must always be
completed. Use the reappraisal step instead: same target, direct evidence in public speaking, a few
seconds, and available to everyone.

### Tier 3 — weaker than expected

**Breathing on its own.** This is the correction to the obvious plan. In the same acute review,
**breathing-based interventions alone produced inconsistent, non-significant results** — active
control of breathing g = −0.46 (CI −2.18 to 1.25, n.s.), attention to breathing g = −0.21 (CI −0.75
to 0.34, n.s.). What *did* work consistently was **combination techniques involving passive
attention** (moderate effects), and active-change combinations were again inconsistent.

The famous breathwork result does not rescue this. Cyclic sighing (two inhales, long exhale) beat
mindfulness meditation on mood and respiratory rate — but that was **5 minutes a day for a month**,
n = 111, and it found **no** change in heart rate or HRV
([Balban et al. 2023, *Cell Reports Medicine*,
doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895)). It is evidence for a
daily practice, not for a single dose before a session. Broader breathwork meta-analysis:
g = −0.32 for anxiety, mostly multi-session, authors explicitly warn about "miscalibration between
hype and evidence" ([Fincham et al. 2023, *Scientific Reports*,
doi:10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-27247-y)).

**Verdict:** a breathing step is defensible only as exhale-emphasised and paired with attention, and
it should be labelled an inference. It is not the strong candidate it looks like. A vocal warm-up
beats it on evidence *and* on relevance.

### Does not survive the check

**Self-imposed deadlines / commitment devices as a performance booster.** The canonical source,
Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), is **retracted — the data were altered**
([Data Colada #139](https://datacolada.org/139); retraction notice on
[doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00441](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00441)). The 2026 direct replication
found that people **do** demand costly self-imposed deadlines, but deadline structure had negligible
effects on performance, lateness and earnings ([Hyndman & Bisin 2026, *Psychological Science*,
doi:10.1177/09567976261460772](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261460772)).

So: a commitment step is fine, but it must be justified as an **implementation intention** (Tier 1),
not as a commitment device. FocusFlight's airplane-mode toggle works as "if I am about to practise,
then I silence the phone" — an if-then plan. It does not work as "I locked myself in." Do not write
copy that claims the latter.

## 8. The settled plan

Seven steps, capped. Anything new must displace something here. One shared ceremony, identical across
all three exercises, same length every session. No artifact, nothing persisted, no step headings —
the components speak for themselves. Separate screens, with the white start button staying in place
and only changing its label, as FocusFlight does across its five screens.

| # | Step | What the user does | Why it is here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Release** | Jaw, neck, shoulders, tongue | Unanimous practitioner consensus, and every clinical source places it first. Cited to the warm-up literature, **not** to the anxiety literature |
| 2 | **Note** — *wide* | Types one short line answering a worry prompt | Ramirez & Beilock 2011, d = 0.57; specificity is the active ingredient, so the prompt must name a worry, not ask for a neutral label |
| 3 | **Purpose** | Taps one of two or three offered purposes | Implementation intention, d = 0.65; choosing a provided option tested equal to writing one out (Armitage & Arden, p = .62) |
| 4 | **Exercise** | One proposed with a reason, two named beneath as one-tap alternatives | Counters the default effect without removing choice. The reason cites the user's own history, falling back to naming what the exercise does. Never an outcome claim |
| 5 | **Speak** | Says one line aloud | Brooks 2014: reappraising arousal as excitement beat calming down on public speaking. Constraint: copy may never tell the user to relax or calm down |
| 6 | **Hum** — *narrow* | Matches a held tone. No mic verification | SOVT warm-up, and an attention task by construction. Beat breathing training on ease of speech (p = 0.030) |
| 7 | **Countdown** — *wide* | Waits through 3-2-1 | Already built. Vohs et al. 2013: a delay after the ritual produced the highest enjoyment |

Steps 2, 6 and 7 are the Three-Minute Breathing Space's wide → narrow → wide alternation, with sound
standing in for breath. Compressing three minutes to ceremony length is inference, defensible because
duration was not a moderator ([Schumer, Lindsay & Creswell
2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6441958/)) but not a matched dose.

### Still open

- **All copy is unwritten.** Held to the same evidentiary bar as the design, per
  `docs/product-spec.md` §0.
- **The visual identity is parked.** The Oct 11 composition rules are void by instruction. Whether a
  figure exists at all is undecided. Imagery is optional and, if used, light; no audience or stage is
  a default rather than a hard rule.
- **Behaviour when the proposed exercise is unavailable.** Conversation has a monthly budget and the
  control reads "Back next month" when spent. Unresolved whether the ceremony still runs.
- **Completion risk is unmeasured.** Seven steps against an ADHD-app benchmark of 8m58s total engaged
  time across a year. No source tells us where ritual benefit and friction cost cross.

## 9. Honest gaps

- **No study tested a pre-session ritual in a speech-practice app.** Everything in §2 is transfer
  from adjacent domains. Flag as inference, per `docs/product-spec.md` §0.
- **The IKEA-effect meta-analysis was read as an abstract only.** Journal of record unconfirmed.
- **Effort-justification classics (Aronson & Mills 1959; Axsom & Cooper 1985) and the effort
  heuristic (Kruger et al. 2004) appear here only via the reference lists of the papers above.** Not
  independently fetched. Do not cite them as primary without reading them.
- **Nobody has measured the trade-off point.** We have evidence that ritual helps and evidence that
  friction costs single-digit percentage points of completion. No source tells us how many steps is
  the optimum, for this population, on a phone.
- **The ADHD session-length constraint is partially unverified** in our own review, and sits in
  unresolved tension with an RCT where gamification *helped* ADHD attention.
- **FocusFlight is one app, observed once, with no data.** Its flow is evidence of a design choice,
  not evidence that the choice works.
- **No source tells us the right number of ritual steps.** Ritual helps; friction costs single-digit
  percentage points of completion. Nobody has measured where those cross on a phone.
- **The vocal-warm-up evidence is in teachers and SLP students, not L2 speakers,** and the strongest
  trial ran six weeks rather than one session. Immediate-dose evidence exists but is small-N and
  partly pre-post without a control arm.
- **The embodiment arm rests on two studies with an outlier removed.** Treat `g = −1.05` as a
  direction, not a magnitude.
- **A breathing step for acute pre-session anxiety is an inference, not a finding.** Breathing alone
  did not reach significance in the only acute review found.
